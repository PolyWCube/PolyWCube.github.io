Dispersed Systems

"Colloid chemistry is the science of neglected dimensions." - Wolfgang Ostwald.

Why does it matter?

A few days ago, during my first physical chemistry and pharmaceutics class, we covered a fundamental concept, which I also personally found interesting: disperse systems.

While it sounds purely academic, it actually explains how many everyday products and medications function. Scientifically, a disperse system consists of one or multiple substances (the dispersed phase) distributed throughout another (the dispersion medium). Studying these systems is essentially about understanding how to make incompatible materials—like oil and water—mix and remain stable over time.

Disperse systems are not unique; they are a fundamental way nature and industry organize matter. On a broader scale, these systems dictate the properties of the world around us. In environmental science, atmospheric fog, smoke, and industrial wastewater are all dynamic dispersions that require precise control for filtration and purification. In consumer manufacturing, the stability of products like commercial paints, structural concrete, and everyday food emulsions (such as milk or mayonnaise) relies entirely on the physics of particle distribution.

In the field of pharmaceutics, however, managing these systems becomes a matter of biological precision.

Discovering a powerful drug molecule is only one of many steps. The real challenge is delivery. Because many modern medical compounds do not naturally dissolve in water, the body cannot absorb them in their raw form. Formulating these drugs into micro- or nano-sized dispersed systems bypasses this hurdle, drastically increasing drug absorption and controlling how fast a medication is released into the human body.

Concept and classification

At its core, a disperse system is a mixture where the dispersed phase is mixed inside a dispersion medium. Unlike a chemical reaction, these substances do not bond together permanently. They remain separate but physically coexist.

The dispersed phase consists of substances that are broken down into tiny particles. It consists of isolated, separated particles or droplets distributed throughout the mixture. The dispersion medium, on the other hand, is the continuous substance that surrounds these particles. It forms an uninterrupted, continuous pathway that entirely surrounds the individual dispersed substance, which is also named the dispersed interface.

However, the relative quantity and physical continuity of the substances determine which component becomes the dispersed phase and which becomes the dispersion medium.

Based on particle size

In pharmaceutical science, the size of the dispersed particles is the single most critical factor. It dictates how a drug behaves visually, how stable it remains on the shelf, and how the human body absorbs it.

Molecular Dispersion

True solution. Completely dissolved and homogeneous.

The dispersed phase are so small that the intermolecular forces between the solute and the solvent are perfectly balanced. They form a true, permanent solution that will never separate. Although classified as a dispersion by particle size, a molecular dispersion is physically a homogeneous true solution where the components completely merge into a single phase.

Some of the properties of a molecularly dispersed system are: clear and transparent; particles (atoms, ions, or small molecules) are invisible even under an electron microscope; passes through semipermeable membranes and filter paper; Highly stable; never settles.

They are commonly found everywhere in our lifes. For example, we have saline solution (0.9% NaCl) used in eye care, dextrose infusions, clear medical syrups; even the air around us is still considered a molecularly dispersed system.

Colloidal Dispersion

The middle ground. Remain permanently suspended.

The dispersed phase is more complex, with different mechanisms of creating a colloidal system. One way is that they form small, distinct clusters (aggregates). However, instead of sinking, they stay floating, scattered dynamically across the middle ground of the medium.

Some of the properties of a colloidal dispersed system are: usually clear to hazy; exhibits the Tyndall effect; driven by Brownian motion; particles resist gravity and rarely settle; cannot pass through semipermeable membranes but pass through regular filter paper.

They are unique and the most fascinating dispersed system among 3 due to the fact that they have many different properties. For example, they are used in targeted drug delivery vehicles (e.g., liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, micelles), or the fact that our blood plasma is a colloidal dispersed system of blood components, as are hydrogels (like gelatin or carbomer gels).

About the clarity of the solution, unlike a true solution, these particles are now large enough to act as tiny mirrors. When light hits them, they scatter the light waves in all directions.

Another thing is that the suspension state of the dispersed phase (colloidal stability) is mostly affected by the force from Brownian motion of the dispersed medium. This acts like an upward force that fights gravity and keeps the phase floating. Additionally, besides that force, the dispersed phase is also affected by the Van der Waals forces and electrostatic repulsion.

Coarse Dispersion

Suspension. Gravity dominates, causing particles to settle.

This is the theoretical cutoff line where gravity starts to drastically overpower thermal movement (Brownian motion).

Some of the properties of a coarse dispersed system are: turbid, milky, or opaque; particles are visible under a standard light microscope; does not pass through regular filter paper; thermodynamically unstable; particles will settle (sediment) or float (cream) over time without stabilizing agents.

They are the other end of the spectrum, mostly used in pharmaceutical suspensions (e.g., liquid antacids like Mylanta, or reconstituted oral antibiotics), emulsions (e.g., topical creams, propofol injectable emulsions).

Looking at the classification table or the simulation, it is easy to fall into the trap of thinking that these particle sizes are absolute, non-negotiable boundaries. If a particle is 999nm, is it definitely colloidal? If it hits 1001nm, does it instantly become a coarse suspension?

The short answer is no. In physical pharmacy, there is no magic number that single-handedly dictates the nature of a disperse system based purely on physical dimensions.

For example, while international textbooks primarily focus on the theoretical 500nm boundary between colloidal and coarse systems, in my country's pharmacy curriculum, we learn a highly practical trick using standard laboratory tools.

Ordinary lab filter paper generally features an effective pore size range of 3000-7000nm. Because of this, we are taught that a quick way to test a dispersion's true nature is simple filtration: colloidal particles will seamlessly pass through this layer of barrier without any pressure, whereas coarse systems (like suspensions and emulsions) will get trapped or clog the pores over time. It bridges the gap between invisible molecular sizes and tangible lab equipment.

Instead, the true behavior of a system is a dynamic result of two crucial factors:

The properties of the components: The molecular weight, density, shape, and surface structure of both the dispersed phase and the medium play a massive role. For example, a heavy, dense metal nanoparticle might start settling (behaving like a coarse system) at a much smaller size than a light, fluffy polymer droplet of the same diameter.

Interfacial forces: The system's identity is defined by the chemical and physical "conversations" happening at the boundary where the two phases meet. Factors like surface charge (Zeta potential), the presence of surfactants, and how much the particles attract or repel the surrounding liquid matter far more than a ruler. A system with small particles can instantly collapse if the attractive forces are too strong, while a system with larger particles can remain beautifully stable if the electrostatic repulsion acts as a perfect shield.

Ultimately, size is just a convenient guideline for us. In the real world, a disperse system is defined by its properties and interactions, not just its parameters.

Based on Physical State

Continued to size is only part of the story. The true identity of each system is dictated by how the medium restricts the particles and how the particles interact at their boundaries. One of the way that directly and greatly affect the interfacial forces between each particle is the physical state of these phases and the medium, which greatly defines the arrangement, and therefore the interfacial forces.

Aerosol

Liquid/solid phase in gas medium

The liquid droplets are highly dynamic because the gas molecules are spaced far apart; the droplets move at high speeds, driven entirely by air currents (convection) and external forces.

Droplets suffer from high surface tension and are prone to evaporation. Over time, smaller droplets tend to evaporate and condense onto larger ones (a process called Ostwald Ripening), or simply vanish into the air.

Unlike liquid droplets, these solid particles cannot evaporate. They remain in the air for long periods if their aerodynamic diameter is small enough to balance out gravity. They are highly susceptible to electrostatic charges—rubbing against the air can cause them to repel or attract each other strongly.

If these particles collide and clump (aggregate), they form dry, brittle clusters that instantly drop out of the air due to increased weight.

Foam

Gas phase in liquid/solid medium

Gas has a much lower density than the surrounding liquid, creating massive upward buoyancy. The bubbles rush to the surface, crowding together.

This system is highly unstable. The liquid walls constantly drain downward due to gravity. As the walls thin out, the bubbles rupture (pop) or coalesce into larger, clunkier bubbles.

The kinetic energy regarding spatial migration drops to almost none. The gas bubbles cannot rise due to buoyancy because the solid walls hold them in a permanent vice grip. Gravity has no effect.

Unlike the gas version of foam, these systems are highly stable and incredibly lightweight. Because the gas cannot escape and the solid walls prevent bubbles from merging, the structure remains porous for decades (e.g., pumice stones or porous polymeric medical implants).

Emulsion

Liquid phase in liquid/solid medium

Droplets move fluidly, constantly colliding due to thermal energy. If the oil is less dense than water, they slowly float upward (creaming); if denser, they sink.

This is the tension of interfacial tension. Because oil and water molecules hate interacting, the droplets are under immense pressure to merge upon collision (coalescence) to minimize their surface area, eventually separating the mixture into two distinct layers unless a surfactant is present to shield them.

The liquid droplets are frozen at their specific spatial coordinates.

This configuration is a brilliant trick used in pharmacy (such as hydrophilic ointments or medicated stick formulations). In a normal liquid emulsion, oil and water droplets constantly collide and separate (coalescence). But by turning the medium into a semi-solid or solid matrix, we kinetically trap the liquid droplets. They can never collide, which means they can never separate on the shelf, ensuring a perfectly uniform dose every time you apply the cream.

Suspension

Solid phase in liquid/solid medium

The particles are heavy and constantly pulled downward by gravity (sedimentation). If the particles are small enough (colloidal range), Brownian motion acts as a safety net, constantly kicking them upward to keep them suspended.

The surface properties (Zeta potential) are critical here. If the particles carry a strong similar electrical charge, they repel each other and stay separated (deflocculated). If the charges are neutralized, they fall into loose, fluffy networks (flocculation) which are easy to shake and redistribute.

Because no motion occurs as both the phase and medium are solid, particles cannot sediment by gravity but are instead locked in place.

This is the crown jewel of modern drug formulation, known as Amorphous Solid Dispersions (ASDs). Many groundbreaking medical molecules are highly crystalline and completely refuse to dissolve in water. Pharmacists dissolve the drug and a polymer together, then instantly freeze or dry them into a solid sheet. The raw, insoluble drug particles are trapped in an unstable "amorphous" state. Because they are locked in a solid structure, they cannot move to regroup into their original crystal form, keeping the drug ready to dissolve the moment it reaches stomach acid.

After breaking down these 8 configurations, you might wonder: Why is the gas phase in gas medium completely missing?

It does not depend blindly on the states of matter, but on whether the substances choose to dissolve or separate at the molecular level:

Gas molecules possess high kinetic energy and instantly intermingle at an individual molecular level. They form a single, uniform phase with zero interfacial tension. Therefore, the gas-in-gas mixtures are true solutions (homogeneous), not disperse systems.

Liquid-in-liquid, however, is not automatically an emulsion. If you mix alcohol and water, they dissolve into each other completely, forming a true solution. It only becomes a disperse system when the liquids are immiscible—like oil and water—forcing the creation of distinct droplets with clear physical boundaries.

Similarly, the same rule applies to solids. In metallurgy, when gold and copper are melted and cooled, they form a homogeneous solid solution (a true alloy). A solid suspension only happens when the solids refuse to blend molecularly, such as trapping insoluble drug particles inside a polymer matrix, locking them as separate physical entities.

Additionally, isn't there is a forth physical state? You might also wonder why the 4th state of matter — Plasma — is completely excluded from the physical state matrix of disperse systems.

The answer is actually fairly simple. It lies in the harsh reality of thermodynamics. Plasma requires extreme energy and temperatures to exist, conditions under which any pharmaceutical active ingredient or biological cell would instantly disintegrate. Furthermore, disperse systems require phases to co-exist without destroying each other, whereas plasma would aggressively ionize any substance entering its field.

Stability

To understand why a disperse system remains mixed or suddenly separates, it is necessary to examine the fundamental law of physical chemistry: systems naturally seek their lowest potential energy state to achieve stability.

By definition, most disperse systems are thermodynamically unstable, meaning they possess an inherent natural tendency to separate over time. This behavior can be explained through a sequential chain of physical events:

The accumulation of surface energy: Imagine a single, large mass of a substance immersed in a medium. In this state, the contact area between the two phases is minimal. However, when that mass is divided into millions of micro- or nano-sized particles, the total surface area increases exponentially. Suddenly, an enormous number of molecules are exposed at the boundary line where the two distinct phases meet. This massive exposure creates a high concentration of free surface energy, leaving the system in an energetically excited and unstable state.

The drive to minimize area: Because nature naturally opposes high-energy states, the system spontaneously attempts to lower its free surface energy. Since the chemical identities of the substances cannot change, the system alters its geometry instead. The particles attempt to reduce their total exposed surface area by drawing closer together.

The mechanics of separation: As a consequence, when moving particles collide due to thermal motion, they tend to adhere or merge. Solid particles aggregate into larger clusters, while fluid droplets fuse into larger spheres. As these aggregates grow in size and mass, the stabilizing effect of Brownian motion diminishes, allowing gravitational forces to dominate. The particles either sink or float, ultimately separating the system back into two distinct, low-energy bulk phases.

The Principles of Stabilization

To counteract this natural tendency toward separation, the free surface energy must be managed through specific physical mechanisms:

Reduction of interfacial tension: Introducing surface-active molecules alters the boundary layer between the two phases. These molecules align at the interface, lowering the tension between the materials and directly reducing the total free surface energy of the system, which diminishes the driving force for aggregation.

Immobilization within a rigid structure: Alternatively, the continuous medium can be transitioned into a solid state. By locking the dispersed particles into fixed coordinates within a rigid matrix, their spatial mobility is reduced to zero. Even though the system retains high surface energy and a theoretical drive to separate, the particles are physically prevented from colliding, thereby kinetically trapping the system in a permanent state of distribution.

Conclusion

Studying disperse systems reveals that the microscopic organization of matter is not governed by static definitions or arbitrary size metrics. Instead, it is a dynamic equilibrium dictated entirely by the laws of thermodynamics, interfacial physics, and phase boundaries.

Whether observing the random collisions of Brownian motion, tracking light scattering via the Tyndall effect, or analyzing the absolute immobility of a solid matrix, the core principle remains uniform: stability is engineered by controlling molecular interactions.

Ultimately, understanding these multi-phase systems allows us to move beyond observing nature's default tendency toward separation. By manipulating surface variables, adjusting phase states, and configuring structural matrices, we essentially act as system programmers for matter—recalibrating physical parameters to force inherently incompatible materials into stable, enduring coexistence.
